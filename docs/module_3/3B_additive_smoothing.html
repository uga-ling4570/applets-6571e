Activity 3B: Additive Smoothing

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a zero count produces a zero probability in maximum likelihood estimation.
  • Calculate add-α probabilities and explain why the denominator increases by αV.
  • Compare how different values of α change a probability distribution.

Background

A bigram model predicts the next word from the previous word. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) divides each word-pair count by the total count for that context. A pair missing from the training data receives probability zero, even when it could occur.

Additive smoothing adds a pseudo-count α to every candidate's count. It gives unseen pairs some probability while keeping the probabilities normalized. Setting α to 1 is called add-one or Laplace smoothing.

Instructions

  1. Inspect the four counts after study, then try α = 0.1, 0.5 and 1.
  2. Compare the charts, table and worked calculation. Track the word with a zero count.
  3. Check your understanding, then use the reflection questions to explain the changes.

Imagine a tiny prediction task after the word study. Its fixed prediction vocabulary contains only language, speech, syntax and meaning. Their observed counts are 6, 3, 1 and 0, so N = 10 and V = 4.

This demonstration shows only that one row of a bigram model. Meaning is in the vocabulary but has not followed study in the training data. It is not an out-of-vocabulary word.

Use 0 to 10 in steps of 0.1. α = 0 gives MLE; α = 1 gives Laplace smoothing.

Reflection & Discussion Guide

  1. Compare meaning at α = 0 and α = 1. Why does an unseen pair receive a positive probability after smoothing?
  2. At α = 1, compare language and syntax with their MLE probabilities. Why can an already observed word's probability increase?
  3. Try α = 10. How does the result illustrate the trade-off between preserving observed frequencies and giving unseen events a chance?
